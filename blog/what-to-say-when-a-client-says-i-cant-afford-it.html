What to Say When a Client Says "I Can't Afford It"

"I can't afford it" is one of the most common sales objections and one of the most misunderstood. What it can really mean, and how to respond without rescuing or pushing.

By Liz Lemarchand · October 26, 2026 · 7 min read

The conversation is going beautifully. Your prospect is open, engaged, nodding along as you describe how you can work together. Then you share the cost, and there's a small pause.

"Honestly? I just can't afford it right now."

And something in you drops.

Within seconds, you find yourself offering a payment plan they didn't ask for. Or a smaller package. Or a discount, just this once. Or you go the other way entirely: "Oh, of course, I completely understand," and you let them go, quietly relieved that the uncomfortable moment is over.

Both responses make complete sense. Neither one actually helps them.

"I can't afford it" is one of the most common objections in sales, and one of the most misunderstood. Sometimes it means exactly what it says. Very often, it means something else. And until you know which one you're hearing, you can't respond honestly.

Why these words land so hard

Before we talk about your prospect, let's talk about you for a moment. Because the reason "I can't afford it" feels so final usually has more to do with your own money story than with theirs.

If you've ever said those words yourself, about a course you wanted, a coach you admired, a program you knew could help, you know how true they feel in the moment. Many of us grew up hearing that money was tight, that we shouldn't ask for too much, that certain things just weren't for people like us. So when a prospect says they can't afford it, it doesn't sound like an objection. It sounds like a fact you recognize.

And suddenly you're in a very uncomfortable position: you're the person causing someone financial strain. For a woman raised to keep everyone comfortable, that can feel almost unbearable. So you rush to fix it.

That rush to discount isn't strategy. It's rescue. And it's worth noticing, because it tends to show up in your pricing long before it shows up on a sales call.

There's a quieter piece too. If you hesitate every time it's your turn to invest in your own growth, you'll find it very hard not to agree with every prospect who hesitates as well. Their doubt will sound like your doubt, and you'll nod along.

I wrote more about this in If You Never Invest in Yourself, It's Hard to Ask Others to Invest in You.

None of this means you've done anything wrong. It just means the first step in handling the "I can't afford it" objection is staying steady enough to actually hear it.

What "I can't afford it" can really mean

When someone says they can't afford your offer, they're usually telling you one of four things. The words are the same. The meaning underneath is very different.

Sometimes, the money genuinely isn't there. They have rent to pay, a family to support, and no room in their budget for this right now. This is real, and it deserves respect. Your job is not to talk anyone into debt or convince them to stretch past what's responsible. A clean, honest no here protects both of you.

Sometimes, they don't yet see the value clearly enough. Any price feels high when the outcome feels fuzzy. If they liked your offer but haven't connected it to a specific change in their life, the number will feel heavy no matter what it is. This is the same gap between interest and decision that causes so many good conversations to stall.

Sometimes, it isn't a priority yet. Most of us find money for the things that feel urgent. That's not a judgment. Your prospect is allowed to have other priorities, and they get to decide what matters most in their life. But it's possible they haven't fully considered what staying where they are will cost them, in time, in energy, in income they aren't earning.

And sometimes, they don't fully trust it yet. They might not trust the process. They might not yet trust you. Or, most often and most quietly, they might not trust themselves. If they've bought programs before and never finished them, the real question isn't "Can I afford this?" It's "Can I afford to let myself down again?"

You'll notice that only one of these four is actually about money. That's why responding to every "I can't afford it" with a lower price so rarely works.

Get curious before you respond

The most useful thing you can do in that moment is resist the urge to solve anything. Instead, ask.

Something as simple as this opens the door: "Thank you for being honest with me. Can I ask, is it the amount itself, or is it more about whether this is the right place to put that money right now?"

That question does a lot of quiet work. It thanks them for telling the truth, which lowers their guard. It doesn't argue. And it gently separates the two things that often get tangled together: the number, and the decision about what that number is worth.

Then follow wherever their answer leads. If they say it really is the amount, you might ask, "If the investment felt manageable, would this feel like the right next step for you?" The answer tells you whether price is truly the obstacle, or whether something else is sitting behind it. If they hesitate, you've learned something important.

If it's less clear, you could ask what they would need to see or understand to feel confident this would be worth it. Sometimes they'll name exactly what's missing. Sometimes, just being asked helps them realize that the hesitation isn't really about money at all.

The tone matters more than the words. This isn't a cross-examination, and it isn't a technique for getting around a no. It's genuine curiosity. Ask, then pause. Let them think. The silence after a good question is where the truth usually shows up.

Responding to what you hear

Once you understand what's really going on, your response becomes much simpler, because you're responding to their actual situation instead of guessing.

If the money genuinely isn't there, honor that. Tell them you appreciate their honesty. If you have a lighter way to work together that would truly serve them, a community, a smaller program, a resource they can start with, you can mention it. But offer it because it fits, not because you're trying to save the sale. And if nothing fits right now, let them go warmly. Someone who leaves a sales conversation feeling respected is far more likely to come back when the timing is right.

If they aren't seeing the value yet, go back to what they told you earlier in the conversation. Use their words. Remind them of the problem they described and connect it, specifically, to how your work helps with that problem. Not a list of features. Just the bridge between where they are and where they said they want to be.

If it isn't a priority, you can gently name what you heard, once, without drama. "You mentioned this has been weighing on you for a couple of years. What would it mean to still be in the same place this time next year?" Then let them answer, and let the decision be theirs. You're not manufacturing urgency. You're helping them look honestly at a choice they're already making.

If it's a trust question, address it directly. Ask what's making them unsure. If it's about the process, explain what working together actually looks like. If it's about themselves, that's often the most tender moment in the whole conversation, and the most important one to slow down for. Reflect back what you hear. Sometimes the most powerful thing you can say is simply, "It sounds like this isn't really about the money. It's about not wanting to start something and not finish it again. Is that close?"

Why discounting rarely solves it

It's worth saying plainly: a discount answers a question your prospect usually didn't ask.

If the real issue is value, a lower price doesn't make the value clearer. It can actually make it murkier, because it signals that the original number wasn't quite real. If the issue is trust, a cheaper version doesn't make anyone feel safer. If it's priority, a smaller number doesn't change what matters most to them this month.

And every time you discount to escape discomfort, you teach your own nervous system that your price is something to apologize for. That lesson follows you into the next conversation, and the one after that.

That doesn't mean payment plans or lower-cost options are wrong. They can be thoughtful, generous parts of how you serve people. The difference is whether you designed them deliberately, or whether you're inventing them on the spot to make a hard feeling go away.

Staying steady when money comes up

The real skill here isn't a script. It's being able to stay calm when money enters the room.

When someone says they can't afford it, notice what happens in your body. The tightness, the urge to fill the silence, the instinct to rescue. You don't have to make it go away. Just notice it, take a breath, and remember that you are not responsible for their budget. You are responsible for an honest conversation.

That shift changes everything. You stop trying to fix their finances and start helping them make a clear decision about them. And that is a far more respectful place to sell from.

You'll also find that "I can't afford it" often travels with its close cousin, "I need to think about it." They come from the same place, and they respond to the same calm curiosity.

You might also recognize yourself in What "I Need to Think About It" Really Means.

"I can't afford it" is not a verdict on your worth, or on the value of what you offer. It's an invitation to understand what's really happening. Some people genuinely can't, and they deserve your respect. Some aren't ready, and they deserve your patience. And some simply needed to be asked one more honest question.

Every one of them deserves the truth. So do you.
