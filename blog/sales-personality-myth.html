The Sales Personality Myth: Why “Natural Born
Salespeople” Don't Actually Exist

Science says the "natural born salesperson" barely exists. Here's what really predicts sales success, and the four beliefs keeping you from building the skill.

By Liz Lemarchand · July 20, 2026 · 9 min read

You have probably told yourself you are "not a sales person."

Maybe you picture the type you think you are not. Smooth. Chatty. Never at a loss for words. Able to walk into a room and work it. You have decided that person exists, that you are not her, and that this means sales will always feel like putting on a costume that does not fit.

Here is what the actual research says: that person you are picturing is not even the best salesperson. She is a stereotype, and the data does not back her up.

✦

The extrovert myth, debunked by science

For decades, the assumption was that extroverts made the best salespeople. Talkative, outgoing, comfortable working a room. It seems obvious on the surface.

Wharton psychologist Adam Grant tested this assumption directly, tracking a group of salespeople over three months. The extroverts in his study generated less revenue per hour than the introverts did. That is not a small, forgivable gap. That is the opposite of what the stereotype predicts.

He was not the only one to find this. A set of meta-analyses covering more than 3,800 salespeople found only a negligible correlation between extraversion and sales performance. A separate meta-analysis of 35 studies involving 4,000 salespeople found the correlation between extraversion and sales performance was statistically insignificant. And a well-known Harvard Business Review study tracking 800 salespeople found extroverts were actually the least effective of the group.

Read that again. The trait we have all been taught defines "a natural" barely predicts success at all.

What Grant's research found instead is that the strongest performers tend to be ambiverts. People who sit in the middle of the introvert-extrovert spectrum, capable of both talking and listening depending on what the moment calls for. Because ambiverts naturally shift between talking and listening, they tend to bring enough assertiveness and enthusiasm to persuade someone without tipping into the kind of over-eagerness that makes people pull back.

This matters for you because most people, statistically, are ambiverts. Not the extreme extroverts the stereotype was built around. Which means the version of "natural sales talent" most of us have been measuring ourselves against was never really the standard to begin with. It was a myth built on a small, unrepresentative slice of the population.

✦

Why the myth persists anyway

If the research is this clear, why does the "born salesperson" idea still feel true?

Because selling, done badly, is loud and visible. The pushy, script-perfect, never-at-a-loss-for-words type is memorable. Confident silence, thoughtful questions, and someone who says "let me think about whether this is actually right for you" are quieter, so they get written out of the story we tell ourselves about what selling looks like.

There is also a nervous system piece underneath this. When you believe selling requires a personality you were not issued, every sales conversation becomes a referendum on whether you are fundamentally the right kind of person. That is an enormous amount of pressure to carry into what should be a simple, human conversation about whether you can help someone. Under that kind of pressure, most people either freeze, over-explain, or perform a version of confidence that was never theirs. That performance is what actually reads as salesy. Not selling itself.

This is the real shift my clients need to make. Selling is not a trait you either have or do not have. It is a skillset, built the same way any skillset is built: through practice, feedback, and unlearning the beliefs that keep you small. Anyone can learn it. But almost nobody can learn it while still believing they need a different personality first.

Here are the four beliefs that keep women stuck at that starting line.

✦

Belief One: "If they say no, I did something wrong."

This belief turns every no into a verdict on you personally. It makes you responsible for an outcome that was never fully within your control.

A no can mean wrong fit. Wrong timing. Competing priorities. Not enough trust built yet. Not enough resources right now. Or simply, no desire, which is a complete and valid answer on its own.

A no is information. It is not a report card on your worth, your skill, or your offer. When you treat every no as proof you failed, you start pre-apologizing for offers before anyone has even said no yet. That pre-apology, not the offer itself, is what reads as salesy. It is the bracing for rejection underneath the words that people actually pick up on.

Belief Two: "If I am kind enough, they will not feel uncomfortable."

This one comes from a good place. You do not want anyone to feel pressured or cornered, so you soften, cushion, and over-explain, trying to manage someone else's feelings before they have even had them.

But buying involves choice, money, risk, and change. Discomfort is sometimes part of an honest decision, not a sign you have done something wrong. Your job is not to eliminate every uncomfortable feeling a person might have while they decide. Your job is to communicate clearly, without manufacturing pressure that was not there to begin with.

There is a real difference between creating pressure and simply being present while someone feels the natural weight of a decision. Confusing the two is what makes clear, direct communication feel dangerous, when really it is just honest.

Selling is not a personality trait. It is what happens when you stop negotiating your worth in the background of every conversation.

Belief Three: "Confidence means having the perfect response."

Many women delay selling until they feel fully certain, fully prepared, fully polished, convinced that confidence sounds smooth and airtight.

It rarely does. Confidence often sounds like "I do not know." "That is not included." "I do not think this is the right fit for you." "Take the time you need."

These are not weak answers. They are answers from someone regulated enough to tell the truth instead of performing a certainty she does not feel. This lines up with something the introvert-in-sales research keeps pointing to as well: real sales success tends to come from genuine confidence and a solid understanding of what the customer actually needs, not from sheer sociability. People can feel the difference between steadiness and a script, and steadiness is what builds trust.

Belief Four: "A good seller can turn any hesitation into a yes."

This belief treats hesitation as a problem to be solved rather than information to be respected. It puts the entire outcome of the conversation on your shoulders, as if the right words could override someone else's actual readiness.

A good seller can help someone understand the decision in front of them. That is the whole job. The decision itself still belongs to the buyer. It always has. When you release the belief that you are personally responsible for someone else's yes, you stop reaching for pressure tactics dressed up as persuasion, and you start communicating from a much steadier place.

✦

The skill anyone can build

None of the four beliefs above are actually about sales technique. They are about self-worth, nervous system regulation, and conditioning that taught you to manage everyone else's comfort before your own, long before you ever had a business to sell.

This is exactly why I do not believe in the idea of a sales personality. I believe in a sales skillset, and skillsets are built, not born. You do not need to become someone louder, smoother, or more naturally outgoing. You need to stop bracing for the worst possible interpretation of a no before it has even happened, and start communicating from a place that is actually yours.

That shift alone changes how every offer, every price, and every follow-up comes out of your mouth.
