Confidence

Nobody Buys From
a Side Hustler

"Side hustle" might describe your schedule. It's rarely the most powerful thing to say about your business.

By Liz Lemarchand · August 1, 2026 · 6 min read

Nobody buys from a side hustler.

That might sting a little, but I had to say it, because I see this constantly.

"I'm a teacher, and I have a side hustle doing coaching."
"I'm a designer, and I have a side hustle selling templates."
"I'm a consultant, and I have a side hustle helping people with wellness."
"I'm a full-time employee, and I have a side hustle doing branding."

And then people wonder why nobody is buying.

Here's the issue. "Side hustle" might describe how much time you have. It does not position you as someone people trust to solve a problem.

People don't buy because you have a side hustle. They buy because they believe you can get them a result.

✦

It's Not Really About the Label

If you strip away the phrase itself, "side hustle" is just a time-management fact. Plenty of successful businesses started part-time. That was never the problem.

The problem is what the label is doing underneath. When you introduce yourself as a side hustler, you're not describing your schedule. You're pre-announcing an exit. You're telling the room, before anyone's even asked, that this isn't the real thing yet, that it's fine if it doesn't work out, that you haven't fully signed up.

Your potential client hears that too, even if you never say it directly. And a nervous system on the other end of a sales conversation is remarkably good at picking up on hedged commitment. If you don't sound sure this is real, they won't be either.

The Label Is Doing a Job for You

Here's the part that's harder to sit with. Calling it a "side hustle" usually isn't an accident of language. It's protection.

If it's just a side hustle, it can quietly fail without meaning anything about you. If it's just a side hustle, you don't have to reckon with what it means to actually want this, to actually charge for it, to actually be seen trying. Keeping one foot labeled "hobby" is a way of never having to find out if you're good enough for the real thing.

That's not a confidence problem you fix by trying to sound more confident. It's a self-worth problem wearing a scheduling excuse.

And it's a sneaky one, because it doesn't feel like fear from the inside. It feels like humility. It feels responsible, even honest. But there's a difference between being honest about your hours and being defensive about your worth, and most of us were never taught to tell those two things apart.

✦

Where the Hedge Shows Up Beyond Your Intro

Once you start noticing it, you'll see this same hedge everywhere, not just in how you introduce yourself.

It's in your Instagram bio, tucked between your day job title and a shrug emoji. It's in the DM where you say "no pressure at all, just let me know" before someone has even asked a question. It's in the way you undersell your own pricing with "but obviously we can figure something out," before anyone has pushed back. It's in the sales call where you mention, unprompted, that this is "still pretty new" for you.

None of these are lies. They're all small acts of self-protection, ways of making sure that if it doesn't work out, you were never fully exposed. But they add up to the same message every time: this isn't something I'm standing behind yet. And a message like that is very hard to sell against, no matter how good the work actually is.

What Full Commitment Actually Sounds Like

This isn't about overclaiming or manufacturing confidence you don't feel yet. It's about noticing the difference between a factual statement and a hedge, and choosing the factual one.

"I have a side hustle helping women with branding" becomes "I help women build brands that actually feel like them."
"I'm still pretty new at this, but I do coaching on the side" becomes "I coach women through the exact thing you're describing."
"No pressure, just let me know if you're interested" becomes silence, or a direct question: "Is this something you want to move forward on?"

Notice none of these reframes add hours to your week. They don't require you to quit your job or pretend your calendar looks different than it does. They just stop doing your client's doubting for them before they've had the chance to decide for themselves.

Your Business May Be Part-Time. Your Positioning Cannot Be.

This is the line worth sitting with. Nobody is asking you to quit your job before you're ready, or to pretend you work full-time hours on this when you don't. Time is just time.

But positioning is a different thing entirely. Positioning is the story you tell about whether this is real. And that story needs to be fully committed, even while your calendar is still split.

A Quick Audit

Next time you introduce what you do, notice which one comes out of your mouth first: your availability, or your value.

"I have a side hustle doing X" leads with your availability.
"I help women do X" or "I work with clients on X" leads with your value.

The first one might be true. It is rarely the most powerful thing to say, and it is almost never what makes someone want to buy.

You don't need a bigger title, a fancier launch, or a personality transplant to be taken seriously. You need to stop introducing your own work as optional before anyone else gets the chance to decide that for themselves.
