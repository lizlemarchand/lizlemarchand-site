Why You Avoid Following Up With Leads
(And It's Not Because You're Lazy)

She said "let me think about it" three weeks ago. You still haven't followed up. Here's what's actually stopping you, and what to say instead.

By Liz Lemarchand · September 22, 2026 · 4 min read

She said she was interested. She asked great questions on the call. She said "let me think about it" and you said "of course, take your time," and that was three weeks ago, and you still haven't followed up.

You tell yourself you're busy. You tell yourself she'll reach out if she's ready. You tell yourself now isn't a good time to bother her. None of that is really the reason, and somewhere underneath the excuses, you already know it.

The real reason is simpler and less flattering: following up feels like begging, and you'd rather lose the sale than feel like you're begging for it.

That single belief, quiet, mostly unexamined, is costing you more revenue than almost anything else in your business. Not your pricing. Not your offer. Not your marketing. The leads who already raised their hand, already had the conversation, already showed real interest, and then just... went cold, because you decided silence was safer than sounding needy.

✦

Let's name what "bothering" actually means here

Somewhere along the way, you picked up the idea that reaching out twice makes you pushy. That a second message means you're chasing someone who's already said no, even when she never actually said no. She said "let me think about it," which is not rejection. It's an open door you're choosing not to walk through.

Here's the reframe that changes everything: following up isn't bothering someone. Following up is doing your job. If a client hired a contractor to renovate her kitchen and the contractor went silent for three weeks after the quote, she wouldn't think "how considerate of him to leave me alone." She'd think he forgot about her, or worse, that he wasn't serious about the work. Silence doesn't read as respectful. It reads as absent.

You're not interrupting her day by following up. You're doing the bare minimum of running a business that people can actually buy from.

✦

What's actually driving the avoidance

Underneath "I don't want to bother her" is usually a much older, much more personal fear: what if I reach out and she says no this time, clearly, directly, to my face? As long as you don't follow up, the maybe stays alive. The lead exists in a comfortable limbo where she still might say yes. The moment you ask again, you risk collapsing that maybe into an actual no, and some part of you would rather protect the fantasy than get the real answer.

That's a completely human impulse. It's also an expensive one, because a "maybe" that never resolves isn't a sale in progress. It's a sale you've already lost, just without the discomfort of knowing it yet.

✦

Practical follow-up that doesn't feel gross

You don't need a script that hedges, apologizes, or buries the ask under layers of "if that's okay." You need language that's Kind, Clear, and True, the same framework that guides everything else you say when you stop seeking permission to take up space. A few that work well:

"Hi Sarah, following up on the proposal I sent last week. I'd love to know where you landed and answer any questions you have." Kind in tone. Clear in what you're asking. True, because you actually want to know.

"Hi Sarah, checking back in on the conversation we had. Is this still a priority for you right now, or has that shifted?" This respects that circumstances change without hiding the question inside an apology for asking it.

If it's been a while and you genuinely haven't heard anything: "Hi Sarah, I want to be direct. I haven't heard back, so I'll close this out for now. If it becomes a priority down the road, reach out and we'll pick it back up." This closes the loop cleanly instead of leaving both of you in limbo, and it often gets a response, not because it manufactures urgency, but because it's honest about where things stand.

None of these hedge. None of them apologize for existing in the other person's inbox. That's the distinction that matters. A permission-seeking message tries to make itself small enough not to be a burden. A Kind, Clear, and True message simply says the true thing, respectfully, and lets her respond to reality instead of to your discomfort.

✦

Build a follow-up habit, not a follow-up mood

Waiting until you feel confident enough to follow up means you'll follow up rarely, because the feeling of readiness doesn't reliably show up on its own. The women who follow up consistently aren't the ones who've eliminated the discomfort. They've built a simple system so the follow-up happens whether they feel ready or not, a note in your calendar three days after every proposal, a five-minute block every Friday to check in on anything still open. The system does the work your mood was never going to reliably do.

Every lead who goes cold without a follow-up isn't a lead who said no. It's a lead who was never actually asked again. That's not a sales skill gap. It's a decision you're making, quietly, dozens of times a month, to let silence do the asking for you. Silence has never once closed a sale. A clear, warm, direct message might.
