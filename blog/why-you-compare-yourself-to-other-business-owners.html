The Real Reason You Compare Yourself
to Other Business Owners

You know comparison steals your joy. You've heard it a hundred times. So why can't you stop doing it?

By Liz Lemarchand · September 22, 2026 · 4 min read

You know you're not supposed to compare yourself to other business owners. You've read the quote about comparison being the thief of joy. You've unfollowed the accounts that make you feel small. And still, somehow, you find yourself scrolling past another woman's launch results, her testimonial screenshots, her "fully booked" announcement, and something in your chest tightens before your brain has even caught up.

You tell yourself to stop. You don't stop. And now, on top of the original sting, you've got a second layer of shame for not being evolved enough to rise above it.

Here's what's actually happening, and it's not a willpower problem. Comparison isn't really about the other person's business at all. It's a scanning behavior, and your nervous system runs it automatically whenever it needs to answer one question: am I safe here?

For most of human history, knowing where you stood relative to the group wasn't vanity. It was survival. Falling too far behind meant real danger, exclusion, scarcity, being left out of protection. Your nervous system still runs that ancient calculation today, except now the "group" is a Skool community or an Instagram feed, and the stakes are a sales call instead of a hunt. The mechanism doesn't know the difference. It just knows: check your position, then check your safety.

So when you see another woman doing what you want to be doing, faster or bigger or with more visible ease, your body doesn't register "interesting data point." It registers "possible threat to my standing," and it responds the way it responds to any threat: contraction, shame, the urge to either withdraw or overwork to catch up.

This is why comparison hits hardest in exactly the areas where you feel least secure. You don't compare your grocery shopping to someone else's. You compare your income, your visibility, your worthiness of success, because those are the places where the old conditioning already planted doubt. Comparison doesn't create the insecurity. It finds the crack that was already there and pours itself into it.

✦

What comparison is actually protecting you from

Underneath the sting of comparison is usually an older, quieter fear: that you might try, fully and visibly, and still not be enough. Comparing yourself to someone further along lets you avoid testing that fear directly. If you stay in the scroll, measuring yourself against her, you never have to find out what happens when you put yourself out there at full volume and see what comes back.

In a strange way, comparison can function as a hiding place disguised as motivation. It feels like you're gathering information, benchmarking, staying sharp. What you're actually doing is keeping your attention pointed outward, at her, so it doesn't have to land fully on your own next uncomfortable step.

And there's another layer worth naming honestly. Comparison thrives in isolation. When you're building alone, with no one who knows the real, unfiltered version of your week, the only data you have access to is other people's highlight reels. Of course the comparison feels brutal. You're measuring your behind-the-scenes against everyone else's best angle, and no version of that math will ever come out even.

✦

What actually loosens comparison's grip

The fix isn't forcing yourself to feel happy for her, though that often comes naturally once the underlying fear settles. The fix is giving your nervous system enough evidence that your own safety and worth aren't actually contingent on your position relative to anyone else.

That starts with proximity to the truth. When you're in real relationship with other women building businesses, not just watching their curated feed but hearing about their slow months, their doubts, their unglamorous Tuesdays, comparison naturally loses its grip. It's hard to feel threatened by someone's highlight reel once you know the whole reel. The scanning behavior calms down because it finally has accurate information instead of a curated illusion to measure itself against.

It also helps to notice comparison the moment it shows up, without immediately trying to talk yourself out of it. Instead of "I shouldn't feel this way," try "there's the old scanning behavior again, checking if I'm safe." That small shift, from judgment to recognition, is often enough to loosen the grip a little, because you stop adding shame on top of an already uncomfortable moment.

Over time, as you build more direct evidence of your own worth, from your own wins, your own clients, your own slow and steady progress, you'll need the comparison less. Not because you've transcended it through sheer willpower, but because your nervous system has finally gathered enough proof that you don't need her results to know you're on solid ground.

You are not behind. You are not too sensitive. You are running a very old survival program in a context it was never designed for, and the way out is not more discipline. It's proximity to the truth, and enough safety, built over time, to stop needing to check.
