Why "Just Believe in Yourself"
Doesn't Work For You

You've said the affirmations. You've journaled the gratitude lists. So why does your business still feel like it's running on fumes?

By Liz Lemarchand · September 22, 2026 · 4 min read

Somewhere along the way, someone told you that confidence is a decision. That if you just believed in yourself enough, said the right affirmations, visualized the right outcome, the fear would lift and the sales would follow. So you tried. You wrote the affirmations on sticky notes. You listened to the podcasts. You told yourself, out loud, in the mirror, that you were worthy of success.

And the fear didn't lift. If anything, it got worse, because now you had a new problem stacked on top of the old one. Not only were you scared, you were failing at not being scared, and that failure felt like proof that something was fundamentally wrong with you.

Here's what nobody told you: belief was never the mechanism. Your nervous system doesn't respond to affirmations the way it responds to actual safety. You can say "I am confident and capable" a hundred times, and if your body is still bracing for judgment, still scanning the room for danger, still tightening every time you have to ask for money, the words don't reach the part of you that's actually driving the fear.

This is why "just believe in yourself" fails so many women who are otherwise sharp, capable, and good at what they do. It treats a physiological response as if it were a thought you could argue with. It's a bit like telling someone with a pounding heart mid-panic attack to simply calm down. Technically true that calm is the goal. Completely useless as a method for getting there.

✦

What's actually happening beneath the fear

When you feel that tightening before a sales call, or the urge to undersell yourself in a proposal, or the impulse to soften your price the second someone hesitates, that's not a confidence gap. That's a nervous system in survival mode, doing exactly what it was trained to do.

For a lot of women, that training started early. Maybe you learned that being too visible, too certain, too much, drew criticism instead of praise. Maybe you learned that your needs were safer left unspoken, that agreeableness kept the peace, that standing firmly in your own worth read as arrogance rather than strength. None of that was a character flaw. It was an adaptation, and it worked, back when the room you were adapting to was smaller and the stakes were different.

The problem is that adaptation didn't get an update. It's still running the same protective program in your business today, years later, in rooms that are no longer dangerous in the way the original room was. Your body doesn't know that. It just knows the pattern: visibility once meant risk, so visibility still triggers the alarm.

That's why affirmations can't fix it. You're not dealing with a belief system. You're dealing with a nervous system that needs actual evidence of safety, not a slogan.

✦

What regulation looks like instead of belief

Real change here isn't about thinking more positively. It's about slowly, deliberately teaching your body that it's safe to be seen, safe to ask, safe to take up space, through repeated, manageable exposure rather than a single dramatic mindset shift.

That might look like sending the higher-priced proposal even though your hands are shaking, and noticing that you survive it. It might look like staying in the room after you say something vulnerable instead of immediately backtracking or over-explaining. It might look like naming the fear out loud to someone who won't flinch, instead of white-knuckling through it alone, because isolation keeps the nervous system on high alert far longer than connection does.

Here's the part that changes everything once you understand it. Each time you do the uncomfortable thing and nothing bad happens, your nervous system quietly updates its evidence file. Not because you decided to feel different, but because you gave your body proof it didn't have before. Do that enough times, in small enough doses, and the discomfort itself starts to feel familiar instead of threatening. You stop needing the fear to disappear before you act. You start acting with the fear still present, and somewhere in that repetition, without ever noticing the exact moment it happened, your comfort zone has quietly expanded to include what used to feel impossible.

That is what confidence actually is. Not the absence of fear. Not a belief you talked yourself into. A wider comfort zone, built one uncomfortable, survived moment at a time.

This is slower than an affirmation. It is also the only version of confidence that actually holds up under pressure, because it's built on real evidence instead of borrowed language. Women who do this work don't stop feeling fear before a big ask. They just stop believing the fear is a stop sign, and their comfort zone keeps quietly growing every time they act anyway.

If you've been trying to think your way into confidence and it hasn't worked, that's not a personal failure. It's proof you were solving the wrong problem. The fix was never a better mindset. It's a regulated nervous system, built through repetition, and that repetition happens faster and holds steadier in community, surrounded by other women doing the same hard thing, than it ever does alone with a journal and an affirmation app.
