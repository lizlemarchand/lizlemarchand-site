The Pricing Formula You Want
Isn't a Pricing Problem

You have enough information to price this. What you're actually looking for is permission.

By Liz Lemarchand · July 25, 2026 · 5 min read

You are staring at a blank field on a pricing page. Your cursor is blinking where a number is supposed to go. You have done the research. You have looked at what other people in your field charge. You have maybe even built a spreadsheet with your monthly expenses, your hours, your margin, your "reasonable" hourly rate multiplied out into a package price. And you still don't know what to type.

So you go looking for a formula. Something that will tell you, definitively, that this number is correct. Cost plus a percentage. Value times some multiplier. A calculator, a quiz, a framework with steps. Something outside of you that can hand you the number so you don't have to be the one who chose it.

This is not a research problem. You have enough information. What you are actually looking for is permission.

✦

Why the Formula Feels Like Relief

There is a real reason this instinct makes sense, and it is not vanity or indecision. Pricing is one of the most exposing acts in business, because it is the one place where you are asked to say, out loud, in a number, what you believe you are worth. Not what the market will bear. Not what feels "fair." What you believe.

Most women were not raised with practice at doing that. You were raised with practice at being agreeable, at not taking up too much space, at deferring to whoever seemed to know better. So when it comes time to name a number, the nervous system does what it has always done in moments of exposure: it looks for an authority to hide behind. A formula is a perfect authority. It's neutral. It's math. If someone pushes back on the price, you can point to the calculation instead of standing behind the choice.

That's not weakness. That's a nervous system trying to protect you from something that genuinely feels dangerous, which is being seen, judged, and possibly rejected, in a single number.

What the Formula Actually Does

Here is the part that doesn't get said enough: the formula doesn't remove the exposure. It just relocates the authority.

Instead of the number coming from you, it comes from the spreadsheet, the industry average, the calculator you found in a Facebook group. But you're still the one who has to type it into the box. You're still the one who has to say it out loud on a sales call. The formula didn't make you safer. It just gave you a place to point when things got uncomfortable, and pointing at something else has never actually made the discomfort go away. It's just delayed it to the moment someone asks you to justify the number and the spreadsheet isn't in the room with you.

This is why so many women who follow a pricing formula to the letter still feel a wave of panic the moment someone asks about cost. The formula was never going to fix that. It was never designed to. It was designed to answer a math question, and the thing you were actually asking was an emotional one.

Real pricing confidence isn't about finding the right number. It's about being able to sit with a number that feels a little too big, and not immediately shrink it.

✦

What This Actually Looks Like

Picture the moment differently. You land on a number, and it feels slightly uncomfortable. Not wildly off, not a number you pulled out of the air to punish yourself, but one that sits a little bigger than what feels "safe" to say. The old pattern is to treat that discomfort as information: it feels too big, therefore it is too big, therefore I should lower it.

But discomfort at that moment isn't a signal that the number is wrong. It's a signal that you're doing something your nervous system has very little practice doing, which is claiming value out loud. That feeling doesn't mean stop. It means you're at the edge of something new, and the edge is supposed to feel unfamiliar.

The women who eventually get calm about pricing are not the ones who found a formula that made the discomfort disappear. They're the ones who learned to let the discomfort be there and hold the number anyway. That's not mindset language, and it's not a switch you flip. It's a skill, built the same way any nervous system skill is built: by practicing staying steady in a moment that used to make you retreat.

Notice It Before You Fix It

You don't have to solve this today, and you don't need another framework to try first. The next time you're looking at a number and reaching for something outside yourself to validate it, just notice that reach. Notice the impulse to Google a formula, to ask five people what they'd charge, to find a calculator that will tell you it's fine. That noticing, on its own, is the actual work. It's the beginning of separating "is this number accurate" from "can I tolerate being the one who chose it."

You don't have to be fully comfortable with your pricing to be doing it right. You just have to be willing to stay in the room with the discomfort a little longer than you used to.
