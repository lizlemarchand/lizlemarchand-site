Your Offer Is Not Obvious
Just Because It's
Obvious to You

Your audience doesn't live inside your head. Here's why a quiet offer is usually a clarity problem, not a sales problem, and the three things people need to understand before they'll say yes.

By Liz Lemarchand · September 2, 2026 · 5 min read

One of the biggest mistakes I see business owners make is assuming people understand their offer because they understand their offer.

But your audience is not inside your head.

They don't know everything you know. They don't automatically understand who your offer is for, what problem it solves, why it matters now, or what changes when they say yes.

And if they have to work too hard to figure it out, they usually won't.

They'll scroll. They'll hesitate. They'll tell themselves, "Maybe later."

Not because your offer is bad.

Because the value was not clear enough, fast enough.

✦

The Gap Between What You Know and What They See

You have lived inside your offer for months, maybe years. You know exactly who it's for, why it works, what's included, what it solves, and what changes on the other side of it. You've said it out loud a hundred times. You've written about it, talked about it on calls, maybe even dreamed about it.

That familiarity is exactly what makes it so easy to overestimate how obvious your offer is to someone encountering it for the first time.

A person scrolling past your post, landing on your page, or reading your bio has none of that context. They are meeting your offer as a stranger. And strangers don't fill in blanks generously. If something takes too long to understand, they don't dig deeper. They move on.

This matters whether you're selling coaching, a course, a service, a digital product, a workshop, a Skool community, or anything else. The format changes. The requirement doesn't: people need to understand three things quickly.

The Three Things People Need to Understand Fast

1. Is this for me?

Make it easy for the right person to recognize themselves.

Not vague, like: "This is for ambitious people who want more."

Specific, like: "This is for women business owners who freeze when it's time to make an offer, say the price, or follow up."

Vague language asks people to do the work of translating your offer into their own situation. Most people won't bother. Specificity does that translation for them. It creates recognition, that flicker of "wait, that's me," which is the moment someone actually starts paying attention.

2. What problem does this help me solve?

Don't hide the real pain behind pretty language.

Name what they are actually struggling with: confusion, inconsistent leads, low engagement, avoiding sales conversations, undercharging, lack of follow-through, a community that isn't converting.

It can feel exposing to name the problem that directly. Softer, more polished language feels safer to write. But polish is often what makes an offer forgettable. People don't pay attention to elegant phrasing. They pay attention when they feel seen.

3. What changes after I say yes?

Features are not enough.

"Weekly calls, templates, modules, and community access" may be useful, but they are not the transformation. A list of deliverables tells someone what they'll receive. It doesn't tell them what will be different in their life or business because of it.

What becomes easier? What becomes clearer? What do they stop doing? What do they finally start doing?

That is what people actually need to understand before they'll say yes.

✦

Give Them a Doorway, Not a Maze

A strong offer does not make people guess. It gives them a clear doorway.

What a clear offer answers

  • Who it's for.
  • What it helps with.
  • Why it matters.
  • What to do next.

Think about the last time you almost bought something and talked yourself out of it. It probably wasn't because the offer was wrong for you. It was probably because you couldn't quite tell, fast enough, whether it was.

When It's a Clarity Problem, Not a Sales Problem

Here's the hard truth: if people keep asking, "So what exactly do you do?" or "Who is this for?" or "How does this work?"... that may not be a sales problem.

It may be a clarity problem.

This distinction matters more than it seems. When it feels like a sales problem, the instinct is to sell harder: more posts, more urgency, more calls to action, more convincing. That rarely works, because you can't convince someone of something they don't yet understand. You end up pushing harder on a message that was never landing in the first place.

When you recognize it as a clarity problem instead, the fix changes entirely. You stop asking, "How do I convince them?" and start asking, "What am I assuming they already know that they don't?"

"You don't need to become louder, pushier, or more aggressive. You need to make the value easier to see."

Where to Start

You don't have to rewrite everything today. Start with one place your offer lives — your bio, your sales page, your last post — and run it through the three questions. Would a total stranger know, within seconds, who this is for? Would they recognize their own problem in your words? Would they understand what changes for them if they said yes?

If the answer is no to any of those, that's not a failure. That's information. It doesn't mean your offer is wrong. It means it isn't visible yet. Those are two very different problems, and only one of them requires you to start over.

Your audience will never know your offer as intimately as you do. Your job isn't to make them work for that understanding. It's to hand it to them, clearly, quickly, and specifically enough that they recognize themselves before they've had a chance to scroll past.
