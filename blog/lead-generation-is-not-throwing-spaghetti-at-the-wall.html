Lead Generation Is Not
Throwing Spaghetti
at the Wall

Lead generation isn't about being everywhere and hoping something works. Here's the real three-question strategy behind consistent, intentional lead generation.

By Liz Lemarchand · September 1, 2026 · 6 min read

A lot of women think lead generation means being everywhere and hoping something works.

Post here. Comment there. Try a freebie. Join another group. Send a few DMs. Make a random offer. Wait.

Then when nothing happens, they think: maybe I'm bad at sales.

But lead generation is not random visibility. It is a real part of the sales process, and it deserves to be treated like one.

Because before someone becomes a client, they have to become a lead. Before they become a lead, they usually need to notice you. And before they notice you, you need to show up where they already are with a message that makes them think, "Oh. She understands the problem I have."

That is not spaghetti. That is strategy.

✦

Why "Just Post More" Isn't a Strategy

Here's what I see over and over with the women I work with. They aren't lazy, and they aren't bad at sales. They're exhausted from doing a version of lead generation that was never built to work: scattering effort across five platforms with no thread connecting any of it, hoping volume will eventually make up for the lack of a plan.

When that doesn't produce clients, the story that shows up next isn't "my approach needs a rebuild." It's "something is wrong with me." That's the part I want to interrupt. The panic-posting cycle isn't a personal failing. It's what happens when nobody ever taught you that lead generation is a system with three specific questions underneath it, not a numbers game you win by showing up more often and hoping harder.

Strong lead generation answers those three questions on purpose, instead of leaving them to chance.

Question One: Where Are My People Already Spending Time?

Not where you feel safest posting. Where your actual buyer persona is looking for answers, asking questions, complaining, searching, comparing, or talking about the problem you solve.

For a lot of women building service-based businesses, that might be Skool communities, Pinterest, Instagram, LinkedIn, Facebook groups, YouTube comments, podcasts, newsletters, local events, or referral networks. Notice that list is about where your people already are, not where you've been told you're supposed to build a following.

If you don't know where they are, you're not doing lead generation yet. You're wandering, and wandering feels a lot like effort while producing very little.

Question Two: What Problem Are They Already Aware Of?

People rarely wake up thinking, "I need a transformational framework." They think things like: I don't know what to charge. I need more clients. I keep getting ghosted. I don't know how to explain what I do. I hate promoting myself. I'm tired of working this hard and still not making enough.

Lead generation works better when your message meets people at the problem they already know they have, not five layers above it and not buried under clever branding.

This is where a lot of well-meaning content misses. It's written from where you are in your understanding of the work, not from where your potential client is standing right now. The gap between those two places is where leads get lost. Closing it isn't about dumbing anything down. It's about respecting that someone has to recognize themselves in your message before they'll trust you with the rest of it.

Question Three: What Is the Next Step?

This is where a lot of people lose the thread. They create helpful content, but never invite. They start conversations, but never lead anywhere. They get attention, but don't give people a path.

A lead generation post should create movement. Comment. DM. Join. Book. Download. Read. Watch. Reply. One clear next step. Not seven. Not "link in bio if you feel called."

Because the goal of lead generation is not just to be seen. The goal is to create the conditions for a sales conversation to begin. That doesn't mean forcing people or manufacturing urgency that isn't real. It means making the path clear for the right people, so the ones who are ready don't have to guess what happens next.

✦

The Hesitation Underneath the Confusion

I want to name something most lead generation advice skips over. A lot of the scattered, spaghetti-at-the-wall approach isn't actually a strategy problem. It's what visibility avoidance looks like when it's dressed up as busyness.

Posting everywhere, vaguely, without a clear next step, can feel like taking action while quietly protecting you from the vulnerability of a real invitation. A clear, specific ask can be rejected. A vague post with no ask can't. If you notice resistance to picking one platform, naming one problem, or stating one next step, that resistance is worth paying attention to before you write your next post. It's usually not a tactics gap. It's the nervous system trying to keep you safe from being ignored, or worse, seen and declined.

Naming that doesn't mean pushing through it by force. It means recognizing the pattern so you can choose differently on purpose, instead of mistaking self-protection for a marketing plan.

What Happens After Someone Raises Their Hand

Lead generation doesn't end the moment someone comments or DMs you. That's actually where a lot of women drop the thread a second time. They put real thought into the invitation, someone responds, and then the follow-through gets vague again. A slow reply. A generic thank you. No clear next step for what happens after the next step.

If question three was about giving people one clear action, treat the response to that action with the same clarity. Someone commented, so you reply and ask a real question. Someone downloaded the freebie, so the follow-up email says exactly what to expect next. Someone booked a call, so the call has a clear purpose instead of a vague "let's chat." A lead is not a finish line. It's the start of a conversation you still have to show up for, with the same intention you used to generate it in the first place.

This is the part that quietly separates a lead generation habit from a lead generation strategy. The habit stops at getting attention. The strategy keeps going until there's an actual conversation happening with a real person who might become a client.

✦

Building a Lead Generation Practice That Actually Holds

So this week, instead of asking, "What should I post?" ask three better questions.

The three questions to ask instead

  • Where are my people?
  • What are they already struggling with?
  • What next step am I inviting them to take?

That is how lead generation becomes cleaner. Less panic. Less guessing. Less shouting into the void. More listening, more intentional positioning, and more clear, honest invitations to the people who are already looking for exactly what you offer.

You don't need to be everywhere. You need to be findable, understood, and clear about what happens next, in the places your people already are. That's not spaghetti. That's a lead generation strategy you can actually repeat.
