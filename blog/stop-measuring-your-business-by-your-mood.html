Self-Leadership

Stop Measuring Your Business
by Your Mood

Your mood is not a business metric. Here's how to stop making reactive decisions from a bad day, and start telling the difference between real data and emotional drama.

By Liz Lemarchand · September 2, 2026 · 6 min read

Some days, your business feels like it's finally working.

Someone comments. Someone asks a question. Someone joins. Someone says, "I need this." Someone seems interested.

And suddenly you feel encouraged. Hopeful, even. Like the thing you've been building is actually landing.

Then the next day comes.

No comments. No replies. No sales. No obvious movement.

And your brain immediately goes: "Maybe this isn't working."

Here's what I want you to catch before it turns into a decision: your mood is not a metric. Your nervous system is not your business dashboard. And one quiet day does not mean your offer, your content, your community, or your business is failing.

✦

Where Reactive Decisions Actually Come From

This is where a lot of women make reactive business decisions.

They change the offer too quickly. They rewrite the whole post. They delete the sales page. They stop talking about the thing they were excited about last week. They decide no one wants it. They start looking for a completely different idea.

Not because the data told them to.

Because discomfort did.

I see this constantly with the women I work with, and I've caught myself doing it too. One quiet day, one silent post, one week without a sale, and suddenly the whole strategy feels wrong. Not because anything has actually changed, but because the feeling of being unseen got loud enough to override everything we know to be true.

This is such an important part of becoming self-led in business.

A self-led entrepreneur does not ignore her feelings. She isn't pretending to be a robot who runs entirely on logic. But she also does not hand the steering wheel to fear, impatience, disappointment, or one bad day. She knows the difference between noticing a feeling and being driven by it.

Data vs. Drama

There is a difference between listening to your intuition and being led by your mood. There is a difference between useful data and emotional drama.

Data says: "My CTA may not be clear enough."
Drama says: "No one wants what I do."

Data says: "This offer needs more explanation."
Drama says: "I'm bad at selling."

Data says: "Engagement was low this week."
Drama says: "I should start over."

Data helps you adjust. Drama makes you abandon.

"Data helps you adjust. Drama makes you abandon."

✦

The Pause Before the Pivot

So before you decide something is not working, pause. This is the part almost everyone skips: the beat between the disappointment and the decision, where you actually get to choose which one is steering.

Ask yourself, before you pivot

  • Have I tested this long enough?
  • Have I repeated the message enough times?
  • Have I clearly explained who this is for?
  • Have I invited people to take the next step?
  • Am I looking at real numbers, or am I reacting to how I feel today?

Because sometimes the post that gets no comments still plants a seed. Sometimes the person who never likes anything is quietly paying attention. Sometimes your audience needs to hear the same truth several times before they recognize themselves in it. Sometimes the thing you think is failing simply hasn't had enough clarity, consistency, or time behind it yet.

This Isn't About Never Changing Course

This does not mean you keep doing something forever if it truly isn't working. Self-leadership isn't stubbornness dressed up in nicer language. Sometimes the offer really does need to change. Sometimes the message really isn't landing, and the data will tell you that clearly, over time, without the drama attached.

The distinction isn't "never quit." It's stop confusing discomfort with evidence.

Here's how to tell the two apart in real time: evidence has a pattern. It shows up across weeks, not hours. It shows up in numbers you can point to, not a feeling you can't shake. Discomfort, on the other hand, usually shows up alone, right after you hit publish, right after the call that didn't convert, right after the day you were hoping would be the one that proved you right. If you can trace the urge to quit back to a single bad hour, that's a feeling asking to be soothed, not data asking to be acted on.

Marketing, sales, visibility, pricing, and leadership will all bring up emotion. That's normal. That's not a sign you're doing it wrong. The work is learning to stay steady enough to make decisions from information, not insecurity.

✦

What to Ask Instead

So today, look at one part of your business where you've been tempted to make a dramatic decision. Maybe it's the offer you were ready to scrap last night. Maybe it's the content pillar you're convinced isn't working. Maybe it's the whole business idea you were one bad Tuesday away from abandoning.

Then ask: "What do I actually know?"

Not what do I fear. Not what do I assume. Not what did my mood decide at 11 p.m.

What do I actually know?

That question alone can save you from changing direction every time your confidence dips. It won't make the quiet days feel good. But it will keep you building on evidence instead of rebuilding, from scratch, every time a bad day convinces you nothing you've made means anything.

The Bottom Line

Your business is not your mood ring. Let the data lead. Let the feelings visit, without letting them drive.

And you don't have to get steady about this on your own, at 11 p.m., staring at a silent inbox, trying to figure out which voice is data and which one is drama.
