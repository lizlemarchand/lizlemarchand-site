If You're Doing Everything Except Talking to Potential Buyers,
That's the Problem

Busy work feels safer than a real conversation. If you're creating nonstop and still not converting, the bottleneck may not be visibility at all.

By Liz Lemarchand · October 7, 2026 · 5 min read

This week I spent a morning going through hundreds of member profiles inside my community and reaching out directly to people I wanted to get to know better.

Not with a pitch. Not with a copy-pasted sales script. Just a genuine invitation to have a conversation.

By lunchtime I had five one-on-one meetings booked. Some of them are with quiet members who have been in the community for a long time but have never posted, commented, or joined a discussion.

And that reminded me of something I see over and over with the women I work with.

✦

Talking to a crowd can feel like talking to no one

There is a reason so many of us lean toward one-to-many communication. Posting, emailing, recording videos, hosting webinars. None of it is necessarily easy, but it often feels less personal than sitting across from one real person.

When you write a post, you are speaking to a crowd. And a crowd can feel strangely like no one at all. There is no single face reacting in real time, no pause while someone decides, no moment where you have to hold your ground.

All of that activity has value. But if you are doing a huge amount of it and still not converting enough people into paying clients, you have to be willing to look at the sales process itself.

Sometimes the bottleneck is not visibility. Sometimes the bottleneck is that you are avoiding direct conversations with the people who are already in your world.

Why the one-on-one conversation feels so exposing

I understand why we avoid it.

When you post to a crowd, nobody is looking you in the eye. If something doesn't land, it disappears into the feed. You never have to hear the silence.

A one-on-one conversation is different. Asking someone what they need opens the door to them saying no. Talking about your offer can trigger every uncomfortable thought you have ever had about being pushy, salesy, annoying, or "too much."

For many women, that fear is not random. It was learned. We were rewarded for being agreeable, for not imposing, for waiting to be asked. So when a conversation asks us to take up space and name what we offer, the body reads it as risk. The nervous system does what it was trained to do: it steers us somewhere safer.

So we stay busy instead

We write another post. We reorganize the classroom. We tweak the branding. We plan another free event. We redo the welcome sequence for the third time.

All of it feels productive. All of it is real work. And all of it is safer than one simple question:

"Would you like to talk?"

If this sounds familiar, you may also recognize yourself in The To-Do List That's Quietly Killing Your Business.

Busy work does not fix a business model

Here is the truth I had to learn myself:

Busy work does not make an unsustainable business model sustainable.

If your business depends on clients, you eventually have to become comfortable having conversations with actual humans. Not because every conversation needs to turn into a sale. It won't, and it shouldn't.

The purpose of a conversation is to understand people better. To learn what they are struggling with and what they actually want. And when there is a genuine fit, to know how to lead the conversation toward a clear next step without apologizing for it.

This is a skill, not a personality trait

Nobody is born knowing how to run a discovery conversation. It is a skill set. You learn it the same way you learn anything: with practice, structure, and support while it still feels uncomfortable.

You do not have to become pushy. You do not have to turn every DM into a pitch. As I wrote in The Opposite of Pushy Is Not Invisible, there is a lot of room between hard selling and hiding.

But you do need to stop hiding behind the tasks that feel safe if the conversations that create revenue are the ones you keep avoiding.

A simple place to start

Look at the people already in your world. Your email list. Your community. The people who liked your post last week, or downloaded your free guide, or showed up to your last event and never said a word.

Pick three. Send each of them a real, personal note. Not a script. Something like: "I'd love to hear what you're working on right now. Would you be open to a short conversation?"

Then notice what comes up in you before you hit send. That reaction is information. It tells you exactly where the work is.
